The economics of a warming world

Growth is not a given.

For seventy years, economists treated growth as the background condition of modern life — a trend you forecast around, not a thing that could be taken away. The newest estimates of what climate change does to output say that assumption was a measurement error.

Built around Bilal & Känzig, The Macroeconomic Impact of Climate Change: Global Versus Local Temperature, Quarterly Journal of Economics 141(2), 2026 — with the studies that agree, the studies that disagree, and the one that was retracted.

01 — The assumption

The quiet premise under every forecast

Almost every projection you have ever read — of pensions, of debt sustainability, of whether your country can afford its health system — rests on a number nobody argues about: the long-run growth rate.

It is easy to see why. World GDP per capita has more than tripled since 1960, from about $3,670 to $11,890 in constant 2015 dollars. Compounding at two or three percent a year is the most reliable fact in modern economic history. Treat it as a constant and you are usually right.

But the constant has been drifting. Growth per head has been slowing for six decades, and the slowdown is not a rounding error — the 1960s ran at more than double the pace of the 1980s and 1990s. That matters here for one reason: it means the trend is not a law of nature. It is an outcome, and outcomes can be pushed on.

02 — The forcing

A century ago, nothing to see

At the turn of the twentieth century the global mean surface temperature wandered around its nineteenth-century average. Warm years and cool years cancelled out. There was no trend to speak of.

By 1950, a nudge

A first shift upward — small enough that it sat comfortably inside the natural variability of the record, and small enough that no economist had any reason to put it in a model.

+0.47°C

1980s: the trend separates

From here the signal pulls clear of the noise. Every decade since has been warmer than the one before it — the 1980s averaged +0.47°C above the 1880–1899 baseline, the 1990s +0.61°C, the 2000s +0.81°C.

+1.4°C

Now

2024 came in at +1.51°C above the 1880–1899 average and 2025 at +1.42°C. In the span of a single working career, the planet has moved through most of the budget that the Paris Agreement was written to protect.

For thirty years, the economics profession looked at that line and concluded it was affordable.

The standard estimate: a permanent 1°C rise lowers world output by 1–3%. Against two percent annual growth, that is about eighteen months of catching up. Once.

03 — The measurement problem

How that number was built

The standard approach compares countries: hot years in Kenya against cool years in Kenya, hot years in Finland against cool years in Finland. To strip out anything happening to the whole world at once — a financial crisis, an oil shock — the models include a time fixed effect for each year.

Run that specification and you get a small, fragile answer: about 1% per shock, implying roughly 3% for a permanent 1°C. Not statistically significant.

The time fixed effect is the climate

That control was supposed to remove nuisance. Instead it removes the subject. Global warming is the thing that happens to every country in the same year. Netting out what is common across the world nets out the common warming — and with it the droughts, extreme wind and extreme precipitation that global temperature predicts and local temperature does not.

7×

Change the thermometer, change the answer

Bilal and Känzig run the same machinery on global mean temperature instead. Same data, same estimator, same standard errors. A permanent 1°C now lowers world GDP by over 20% — roughly seven times the conventional figure, and this time significant at the 5% level.

The disagreement in this literature was never really about economics. It was about which temperature you put on the right-hand side.

04 — The shock

−2%

Year zero

Take a 1°C global temperature shock. In the year it lands, world GDP per capita falls by 2–3%. Bad, but survivable — the size of an ordinary recession.

It does not stop

This is where the intuition breaks. A weather shock should wash out: a bad harvest, a rebuild, back to trend. Instead the loss keeps deepening, year after year, because the temperature itself stays elevated — a decade later the shock has still not fully decayed.

−12 to −16%

Six years in

By year six, world GDP per capita is down 12–16% against where it would have been. That is not a bad quarter. That is a lost decade, arriving quietly, distributed across every economy at once.

The peak, and no recovery

The effect peaks at −14% in year six, with a 95% confidence interval running from −6% to −22%. Ten years out the economy has still not mean-reverted. There is no snap back in this data.

−18%

And it holds over 160 years

Run the same estimate on the Barro–Ursúa data — 43 countries back to 1860, through two world wars and the Depression — and the peak is −18% at year five, CI −6% to −30%. A different century, the same shape.

>20%

What a permanent 1°C means

These are responses to a temporary shock that fades. Climate change is not temporary. Converted to a permanent 1°C rise, the estimate is a loss of more than 20% of world GDP — per degree.

05 — Transmission

Why it compounds instead of clearing

A level effect you can grow out of. A growth effect you cannot. The difference is whether the damage lands on this year's output or on the machinery that produces every future year's output.

The paper opens up the components of GDP, and the answer is unambiguous: the damage lands on the machinery. Investment falls. The capital stock falls. Total factor productivity and labour productivity both fall significantly — and the effects strengthen with time, from −2% on impact to over −10% after four years.

That kills the most comforting story in disaster economics — the idea that rebuilding after a catastrophe is a stimulus, that the broken window pays for itself. It does not. In the authors' words, disasters associated with global warming do not stimulate growth. National income, productive capital and investment all dwindle together.

06 — The bill already paid

So why has nobody noticed?

If the effect is this large, it should be obvious. It isn't — and the reason is that climate change arrives in increments. A tenth of a degree a year hides comfortably inside the ordinary variability of the business cycle. There is never a moment where the damage announces itself.

−⅓

It has been coming out of growth all along

The paper's counterfactual is blunt. Since 1960, warming has caused a gradual reduction in the annual world growth rate that reaches one third of baseline by 2019. Not one third of one year's growth — one third of the rate, compounding.

+20%

The gap you are looking at

Had no warming occurred between 1960 and 2019, world GDP per capita would be more than 20% higher today. The shaded wedge is not a forecast. It is income that has already not happened — and it maps onto exactly the growth slowdown we started with.

Growth did not stop. It was quietly taxed, at a rate nobody voted for and no national accounts line reports.

Read the confidence interval rather than the point estimate, because the point estimate is not the argument. Even the optimistic end of the band — a 29% loss — is a permanent contraction with no precedent in peacetime.

The authors' own benchmark: losses comparable to the 1929 Great Depression, but experienced permanently. Roughly ten times the cost of abandoning international trade altogether.

One clarification the paper is careful about, and so is this page: these are losses relative to a baseline that still has growth in it, not absolute declines from today. The world of 2100 is not necessarily poorer than the world of 2026. It is dramatically poorer than the world of 2100 would otherwise have been — and at the top of the confidence band, the distinction stops being reassuring.

08 — The price

What a ton of CO₂ actually costs

The social cost of carbon is the whole argument compressed into one number: the damage done by emitting one more ton. It is what carbon taxes are calibrated against and what regulators use to decide whether a rule pays for itself.

The conventional high-end figure is Rennert et al.'s $185 a ton. Bilal and Känzig get $1,207 — with a 95% interval of $399 to $2,015. The lower bound of the new estimate is more than double the old central value.

The middle bar is the one to sit with. It is the same structural model, re-estimated on local temperature: $149 a ton, a 5% welfare cost, neither figure statistically significant. Every difference between $149 and $1,207 comes from the choice of thermometer.

The practical consequence is the paper's closing point, and it is not a call for global coordination. At these damages, unilateral decarbonisation is cost-effective for a large economy acting alone — the United States included. The free-rider argument that has framed climate policy for three decades depends on damages being small. If they are not, it dissolves.

09 — How sure is any of this

The honest version

A page that only showed you the largest estimate would be doing the same thing it accuses the old literature of. So here is the spread — including the paper that did not survive.

The retraction

In April 2024, Kotz, Levermann and Wenz published an estimate in Nature that the world was already committed to a 19% income reduction by 2049 — $38 trillion a year. It was everywhere.

In December 2025 the authors retracted it. Critics found anomalies in the Uzbekistan data that, once removed, brought the estimate back into line with the previous literature, and argued the uncertainty had been understated badly enough to render the results statistically insignificant. The authors' own reanalysis gives 17% rather than 19%, with a wider band — and they judged the corrections too substantial for a correction notice.

It belongs on this page precisely because it cuts against the argument. This is a young, contested literature where headline numbers move by factors of ten and prominent results get withdrawn.

The serious middle

The most careful dissent is Nath, Ramey and Klenow, who reject both poles. Warming, they find, has effects on growth that are persistent but not permanent. Their projections come out 3–5× larger than pure level-effect estimates and 2–4× smaller than permanent-growth-effect estimates.

That is a real disagreement with the headline number on this page, and it should be read as one. But note where the middle sits. Even the compromise position is several times the figure that a generation of climate policy was calibrated on.

What the estimates cannot see

Two limitations the authors state plainly. First, all of this is identified off moderate short-run variation, so adaptation is captured only imperfectly — by the global-temperature approach and the conventional one alike. If adaptation accelerates beyond anything yet observed, these figures are upper bounds. The stability of the estimates across very different time periods suggests it has not so far.

Second, the top-down approach buys breadth at the cost of detail: it captures total damage without identifying which channel produced it, and it says little about who bears it. Distributional consequences are almost certainly worse than the global average implies.

Set against all of that, one thing has moved decisively and in one direction. The floor has risen. The disagreement is now between "several times worse than we thought" and "an order of magnitude worse than we thought" — and the old 1–3% consensus has no serious constituency left.

Note which perils are doing the work. Droughts, extreme wind and extreme precipitation are precisely the events the paper shows global temperature predicts and local temperature does not. The mechanism the econometrics infers is the one the loss ledgers are recording.

And insured losses are the small number. They exclude everything uninsured — which, globally, is most of it.

The question was never whether the climate would change the economy. It is whether growth — the thing every plan, pension and projection quietly assumes — survives it in the form we have been assuming.

On the current evidence, that assumption is not one you get for free.

The upshot

Three things to take away

One: the old numbers were measuring the wrong variable. A 1–3% loss per permanent degree was not a finding about the climate. It was an artefact of controls that removed the climate from the regression. Change the thermometer and the answer moves by a factor of seven, using the same data and the same estimator.

Two: the damage lands on growth, not on the level. Investment, capital and productivity all fall, and the effects deepen for years. That is the difference between a shock you grow out of and one you compound. It has been running since 1960 — costing, by these estimates, a third of the world growth rate by 2019.

Three: uncertainty is not the same as reassurance. The error bars here are genuinely wide, one prominent paper has been retracted, and careful researchers put the number several times lower than the headline. But the band is two-sided. Its lower end is still a permanent loss on the scale of the Great Depression, and its upper end — a 77% loss by 2100 — is not a scenario any economy has a plan for.

Growth is not a law. It is an outcome of capital, productivity and a climate stable enough for both to accumulate. Two of those three are now measurably under pressure from the third.

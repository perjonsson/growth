The economics of a warming world

Growth is not a given.

Your pension, your country's budget, your children's prospects — all of it assumes the economy keeps growing. New research says we measured the climate's bill with a broken instrument. The real one has already come due.

A 7-minute read, built on Bilal & Känzig, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2026 — with the studies that disagree, and the one that was retracted.

↓ Scroll

01 — The promise

We treat growth like gravity

Something that was here yesterday, is here today, and can be relied on tomorrow.

And why not. The average person on Earth earns about three times what they did in 1960. Every pension, every national budget, every mortgage you will ever be offered is built on the assumption that this keeps going.

But look at the actual pace. It has been falling for sixty years. Growth is not a law of nature. It is a result — and results can be taken away.

02 — Meanwhile

1900: nothing to see

Warm years, cool years. They cancel out. No trend, and no reason for any economist to think about the weather.

A slight lift — still small enough to hide inside the ordinary year-to-year wobble.

1980: the line breaks away

Now it is unmistakable. Every decade since has been hotter than the one before it. No exceptions.

+1.4°C

Today

Inside one working lifetime, we have spent most of the budget the Paris Agreement was written to protect.

For thirty years, economists looked at that line and called it affordable.

The textbook number: one degree, permanently, costs the world about 1–3% of its output. At normal growth you would earn that back in eighteen months. Once.

03 — The mistake

Then someone checked the thermometer

That comfortable number came from comparing countries. And in the comparison, something went missing.

To measure fairly, the standard method removes anything that hit the whole world in a given year — a financial crash, an oil shock — so it doesn't get blamed on the weather. It does that by subtracting the world average for each year.

The argument in this field was never really about economics. It was about which thermometer you read.

The old answer

Measure each country against its own weather and one degree, permanently, costs about 3% of world output. Small — and shaky enough that it could just be chance.

7×

The same maths, one change

In 2026, two economists reran it against the temperature of the planet. Same countries, same years, same method. One degree now costs more than 20% of world output — seven times more, and this time solid enough to bet on.

04 — The damage

−2%

A hot year lands

The world loses about 2% of its income. Unpleasant, but ordinary — the size of a normal recession.

Then it keeps falling

This is the part nobody expected. A bad year should wash out: rough harvest, rebuild, back to normal. Instead the hole gets deeper every year afterwards.

−14%

Six years later, still falling

The loss bottoms out around 14% — a lost decade from one hot year. Ten years on, there is still no recovery.

>20%

And it holds for 160 years

Run it back through two world wars and the Great Depression and the shape is the same. Because the heat doesn't leave, the real figure is over 20% — for every degree.

05 — Why it sticks

The damage hits the machinery

There is a difference between losing this year's harvest and losing the tractor.

Break down where the loss lands and it is never the harvest. Investment falls. Factories, roads and equipment don't get replaced. Workers produce less from the same tools — and all of it gets worse over the following years, not better.

Which kills the one comforting story in disaster economics: the idea that rebuilding after a catastrophe is good for business. It isn't. Nothing here rebounds.

06 — The twist

So why has nobody noticed?

Because it arrives a tenth of a degree at a time. There is never a morning where the damage announces itself. It just looks like a slightly disappointing decade — again and again.

−⅓

It has been running since 1960

By 2019, warming had cut roughly a third off the world's growth rate. Not a third of one year. A third of the rate, every year, compounding.

+20%

This gap is not a forecast

Without the warming since 1960, the average person on Earth would be more than 20% richer today. The orange line is the world we would be living in. The shaded wedge already happened.

Growth didn't slow down. It was being quietly taxed — at a rate nobody voted for, that appears on no receipt.

07 — The bill

What the rest of the century costs

How much worse off that feels
Felt today, not in 2100 — and rising toward −56% as it gets hotter.

The authors reach for the only comparison that fits: the Great Depression. Except this one doesn't end.

One honest caveat. These are losses against a future that still has growth in it — so 2100 isn't necessarily poorer than today. It is dramatically poorer than 2100 should have been. At the bad end of the range, that distinction stops being comforting.

Convert all of it into one number — what it costs the world when anyone burns one more ton of CO₂ — and you get $1,207. Governments currently use $185.

Look at the middle bar. That is the same model, run on the old thermometer: $149. Everything between $149 and $1,207 is the measurement, not the world.

Which quietly detonates thirty years of climate politics. The reason to wait for everyone else was that acting alone doesn't pay. At these numbers, it pays — for any large country, on its own, out of pure self-interest.

08 — The catch

How sure is anyone about this?

Less sure than the headline suggests. Here is the whole spread, including the study that fell apart.

Three reasons to stay sceptical

A big one was withdrawn. In 2024 a Nature paper put the damage at 19% by 2049 and went everywhere. In December 2025 its own authors retracted it — bad data for one country, and uncertainty that had been undersold.

Serious people disagree. The most careful rebuttal finds the damage is lasting but not permanent, landing several times below the headline here.

Nobody can see the future. These estimates are built on heat we have already lived through, so they can't fully capture how well we might adapt. Nor do they show who gets hurt — which is never evenly spread.

But notice what the disagreement is now about. Not whether the damage is large. The argument is between "several times worse than we thought" and "ten times worse than we thought." The comfortable 1–3% has nobody left defending it.

If you remember one thing

The world economy is already about 20% smaller than it should be, because of warming that has already happened.

Not a 2100 projection. Not a worst case. The bill for 1960–2019, and it is already paid. Every further degree takes roughly another fifth.

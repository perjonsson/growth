The economics of a warming world

Growth is not a given.

Your pension, your country's budget, your children's prospects — all of it assumes the economy keeps growing. New research says we measured the climate's bill with a broken instrument. The real one has already come due.

A 7-minute read, built on Bilal & Känzig, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2026 — with the studies that disagree, and the one that was retracted.

↓ Scroll

01 — The promise

We treat growth like gravity

Something that was here yesterday, is here today, and can be relied on tomorrow.

And why not. The average person on Earth earns three times what they did in 1960. Every pension and every budget is built on that continuing.

But look at the actual pace. It has been falling for sixty years. Growth is not a law of nature. It is a result — and results can be taken away.

02 — Meanwhile

1900: nothing to see

Warm years, cool years. They cancel out. No trend, and no reason for any economist to think about the weather.

1950: a nudge

A slight lift — still small enough to hide inside the ordinary year-to-year wobble.

+0.5°C

1980: the line breaks away

Now it is unmistakable. Every decade since has been hotter than the one before it. No exceptions.

+1.4°C

Today

Inside one working lifetime, we have spent most of the budget the Paris Agreement was written to protect.

For thirty years, economists looked at that line and called it affordable.

The textbook number: one degree, permanently, costs the world about 1–3% of its output. At normal growth you would earn that back in eighteen months. Once.

03 — The mistake

Then someone checked the thermometer

Imagine measuring how fast your children are growing by only ever comparing them to each other.

Both of them grow six inches. The gap between them barely moves. Your measurements say nothing happened.

That is, almost exactly, how the cost of climate change was calculated. To be fair between countries, the standard method strips out whatever hit the whole world that year — then hunts for the damage in what is left.

The argument in this field was never really about economics. It was about which thermometer you read.

The old answer

Measure each country against its own weather and one degree, permanently, costs about 3% of world output. Small — and shaky enough that it could just be chance.

7×

The same maths, one change

In 2026, two economists reran it against the temperature of the planet. Same countries, same years, same method. One degree now costs more than 20% of world output — seven times more, and this time solid enough to bet on.

04 — The damage

−2%

A hot year lands

The world loses about 2% of its income. Unpleasant, but ordinary — the size of a normal recession.

Then it keeps falling

This is the part nobody expected. A bad year should wash out — rough harvest, rebuild, back to normal. Instead the hole gets deeper every year afterwards.

−14%

Six years later, still falling

The loss bottoms out near 14% — a lost decade from one hot year. Ten years on, still no recovery.

>20%

And it holds for 160 years

Run it back through two world wars and the Depression: same shape. And because the heat never leaves, the real figure is over 20% — per degree.

05 — Why it sticks

The harvest, or the tractor

Every disaster takes one of two things from you. Only one of them grows back.

Which kills the one comforting story in disaster economics — that rebuilding is good for business. It isn't. Nothing here rebounds.

06 — The twist

So why has nobody noticed?

Because it arrives a tenth of a degree at a time. There is never a morning where the damage announces itself. It just looks like another slightly disappointing decade.

−⅓

It has been running since 1960

By 2019, warming had cut roughly a third off the world's growth rate. Not a third of one year — a third of the rate, every year, compounding.

+20%

This gap is not a forecast

Without the warming since 1960, the average person on Earth would be more than 20% richer today. The orange line is the world we should be living in.

Growth didn't slow down. It was being quietly taxed — at a rate nobody voted for, that appears on no receipt.

07 — The bill

What the rest of the century costs

Run it forward to about 3°C by 2100 — where current policy points.

−53%
Income per person by 2100
Against a world that never warmed. Range: −29% to −77%.
−51%
Roads, factories, equipment
Half the things we would otherwise have built.
−35%
How much worse off that feels
Felt today, not in 2100 — heading for −56%.

The authors reach for the only comparison that fits: the Great Depression. Except this one doesn't end.

One honest caveat: these are losses against a future that still has growth in it. 2100 isn't poorer than today — it is dramatically poorer than 2100 should have been.

Boil it down to one number — what the world loses when anyone burns one more ton of CO₂ — and you get $1,207. Governments use $185.

The middle bar is the same model on the old thermometer: $149. Everything between $149 and $1,207 is the measurement, not the world.

Which quietly detonates thirty years of climate politics. The reason to wait for everyone else was that acting alone doesn't pay. At these numbers it pays — for any large country, alone, out of pure self-interest.

08 — The catch

How sure is anyone about this?

Less sure than the headline suggests. Here is the spread — including the study that fell apart.

Three reasons to stay sceptical

A big one was withdrawn. A 2024 Nature paper put the damage at 19% by 2049 and went everywhere. In December 2025 its own authors retracted it: bad data for one country, uncertainty undersold.

Serious people disagree. The most careful rebuttal finds the damage lasting but not permanent — several times below the headline here.

Nobody can see the future. They are built on heat we have already lived through, so they cannot fully capture how well we might adapt — or show who gets hurt, which is never even.

But notice what the disagreement is now about. Not whether the damage is large — only whether it is several times worse than we thought, or ten times. Nobody is left defending the comfortable 1–3%.

09 — Meanwhile, in the real world

Somebody is already paying

Models are arguable. Insurance payouts are not — a cheque was written.

$107bn
Insured disaster losses in 2025
And that is the small number. Most of the world's damage isn't insured at all.
92%
From fire, flood and storm
A record share — and exactly the disasters the planet's temperature predicts.

Growth was never a law. It is what you get when capital, know-how and a stable climate compound together for a long time. We spent seventy years assuming the third one was free.

If you remember one thing

The world economy is already about 20% smaller than it should be, because of warming that has already happened.

Not a 2100 projection. Not a worst case. The bill for 1960–2019, and it is already paid. Every further degree takes roughly another fifth.
